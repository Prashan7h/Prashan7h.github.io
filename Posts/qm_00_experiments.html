Where classical physics broke

  1. Hot objects: classical theory predicts infinite radiated energy (ultraviolet catastrophe).
  2. Light on metals: electron energy depends on colour, not brightness; sharp threshold.
  3. Atoms: an orbiting electron should radiate and collapse in ~$10^{-11}$ s and give a continuous spectrum. Atoms are stable, with sharp lines.
  4. Cold solids: heat capacities fall to zero as $T\to0$ instead of staying at $3k_B$ per atom.
  5. Particles vs waves: light arrives in lumps; electrons interfere.
  6. Magnetic atoms: angular momentum along any axis takes only discrete values.
  7. Correlations: entangled pairs beat every local classical model.
  8. Identical particles: photons and electrons have no individual identity.

Timeline

1

Blackbody radiation

Lummer–Pringsheim, Rubens–Kurlbaum 1899–1900 · Planck 1900energy quanta
Classical
Every cavity mode gets $k_BT$, and there are ∝ $\nu^2$ modes, so the energy density grows without bound at high frequency.
Observed
The spectrum peaks and falls off; the peak moves to shorter λ as $T$ rises. Today's best blackbody: the cosmic microwave background (COBE 1990, $T=2.725$ K).
Lesson
Oscillators exchange energy only in steps $h\nu$, so high-frequency modes are frozen out. The same idea explains heat capacities vanishing as $T\to0$ (Einstein 1907).
$$u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}\ \xrightarrow{\ h\nu\ll k_BT\ }\ \frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}k_BT$$
2

Photoelectric effect

Hertz 1887 · Lenard 1902 · Einstein 1905 · Millikan 1916photons
Classical
Brighter light carries more energy, so it should eject faster electrons; any colour should work after a delay.
Observed
Electron energy depends only on frequency; below a threshold $\nu_0$ nothing comes out however bright; no delay. Intensity changes only the number of electrons.
Lesson
Light itself comes in quanta (photons), each giving its whole energy $h\nu$ to one electron.
$$eV_s=K_{\max}=h\nu-W,\qquad \nu_0=W/h$$
3

Compton scattering

A. H. Compton 1923photon momentum
Classical
An EM wave shakes the electron at its own frequency, so scattered light keeps the same wavelength.
Observed
A second, longer-wavelength peak whose shift depends only on angle, not on the target material.
Lesson
Photons carry momentum $p=h/\lambda$; the shift is exactly a relativistic billiard-ball collision.
$$\lambda'-\lambda=\frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\theta),\qquad \lambda_C=\frac{h}{m_ec}=2.43\ \text{pm}$$
4

Atomic spectra & stable atoms

Balmer 1885 · Rutherford 1911 · Bohr 1913discrete levels
Classical
Rutherford's atom (electron orbiting a tiny nucleus) radiates continuously and spirals in within ~$10^{-11}$ s.
Observed
Atoms are stable and emit only sharp, element-specific lines that fit simple integer formulas.
Lesson
Bound systems have discrete energies; a photon carries the difference. Stability comes from the uncertainty principle: confining the electron costs kinetic energy.
$$\frac1\lambda=R_\infty\Big(\frac1{n_f^2}-\frac1{n_i^2}\Big),\quad R_\infty=1.097\times10^7\ \text{m}^{-1},\quad h\nu=E_i-E_f$$
5

Franck–Hertz experiment

J. Franck & G. Hertz 1914levels via collisions
Classical
Electrons should lose energy to atoms in any amount, so current rises smoothly with voltage.
Observed
Sharp current drops at 4.9 V spacing; the vapour then glows at 254 nm $=hc/4.9$ eV.
Lesson
Discrete levels show up in collisions too, not just in light: atoms accept energy only in fixed amounts.
6

Double slit with single electrons

Davisson–Germer 1927 · Jönsson 1961 · Tonomura 1989amplitudes & Born rule
Classical
Particles go through one slit or the other: two overlapping bumps. Waves would arrive spread out, not as dots.
Observed
Single dots, one at a time, building fringes with spacing $\lambda L/d$, $\lambda=h/p$. Davisson–Germer: 54 eV electrons on nickel diffract at 50°, matching $\lambda=1.67$ Å. Determining which slit erases the fringes.
Lesson
Amplitudes add, then square (Born rule); outcomes are random; which-path information destroys interference. Seen with neutrons, atoms, even $C_{60}$.
$$P(x)=|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2=|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+2\,\text{Re}\,\psi_1^*\psi_2$$
7

Single-photon Mach–Zehnder

Grangier, Roger & Aspect 1986superposition of paths
Classical
A particle takes one arm (no interference); a wave splits and could trigger both detectors at once.
Observed
At one splitter the photon is found on one side only, yet in the full interferometer the two paths interfere.
Lesson
The photon is in a superposition of paths, and relative phases are physical. Elitzur–Vaidman: a live bomb in one arm is found without being touched (¼ of the time; near 100% with the Zeno trick).
$$P(D_0)=\cos^2\frac\delta2,\qquad P(D_1)=\sin^2\frac\delta2$$
8

Stern–Gerlach

O. Stern & W. Gerlach 1922spin & measurement
Classical
Atomic magnets point in random directions, so deflections should be continuous.
Observed
Exactly two outcomes, along any axis you choose.
Lesson
Intrinsic spin ½ (a two-state system); quantized angular momentum; measurement changes the state; $S_x$ and $S_z$ are incompatible.
$$|z;+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|x;+\rangle+|x;-\rangle\big),\qquad [\hat S_x,\hat S_z]=-i\hbar\hat S_y\neq0$$
9

Bell tests

EPR 1935 · Bell 1964 · Aspect, Grangier & Roger 1982 · loophole-free 2015entanglement
Classical
EPR: perfect correlations mean outcomes were fixed in advance by local hidden variables. Then the CHSH combination $S\le2$.
Observed
$S\approx2.7$, violating the bound by tens of standard deviations. 2015 experiments (Delft, NIST, Vienna) closed the locality and detection loopholes; Nobel 2022.
Lesson
Nature is not locally realistic. Entanglement is a resource (teleportation, QKD) but still can't send signals faster than light.
$$S=|E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a',b')|\le2\ \ (\text{local}),\qquad S_{\rm QM}^{\max}=2\sqrt2$$
10

Hong–Ou–Mandel

C. K. Hong, Z. Y. Ou & L. Mandel 1987identical particles
Classical
Two independent particles exit on opposite sides half the time.
Observed
Identical photons arriving together always leave by the same port.
Lesson
"Both transmitted" and "both reflected" are indistinguishable and their amplitudes cancel. Bosons bunch; fermions would always separate (Pauli).
$$A_{\rm coinc}=t^2+r^2=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big)^2+\Big(\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\Big)^2=0$$
11

Alpha decay

Geiger & Nuttall 1911 · Gamow, Gurney & Condon 1928tunneling
Classical
An α particle with 4–9 MeV can never cross a ~25 MeV Coulomb barrier.
Observed
Nuclei emit α particles with half-lives from $10^{-7}$ s to $10^{17}$ s, and $\log\tau$ is linear in $Z/\sqrt E$ (Geiger–Nuttall).
Lesson
Wavefunctions penetrate classically forbidden regions with exponential sensitivity. Same physics: scanning tunnelling microscope, fusion in stars.
$$T\sim e^{-2\theta},\quad\theta=\frac1\hbar\int_R^{b}\sqrt{2m(V-E)}\,dr\ \Rightarrow\ \ln\tau\approx a+\frac{bZ}{\sqrt E}$$
+

Further confirmations

  • Aharonov–Bohm (1959; Tonomura 1986): electron fringes shift around a shielded solenoid where $\vec B=0$, so potentials and phases are physical.
  • Lamb shift (1947) and electron $g-2$: tiny shifts that need quantum fields (QED).
  • Zeeman & fine structure: spin–orbit coupling, $g=2$, relativistic corrections.
  • Quantum Zeno effect (Itano 1990): frequent measurement freezes a transition.
  • Decoherence (Haroche 1996): a cavity "cat state" observed losing coherence, showing why the everyday world looks classical.

The minimal set. Photoelectric (quanta) + Franck–Hertz (levels) + single-electron double slit (amplitudes, Born rule, complementarity) + Stern–Gerlach sequences (spin, measurement, non-commutation) + a Bell test (entanglement) together demand every axiom of quantum mechanics. Exchange symmetry needs Hong–Ou–Mandel as well.
